\section{Conclusion}

Bhoonidhi solves discovery. Its STAC-based API is real, modern engineering, and
this report makes no complaint about it, nor about the requirement to hold a
token.

What it does not do is \emph{compute}. It stores the footprint polygon of every
scene and permits one question of that geometry: does it intersect mine? The
audit establishes this from NRSC's own published specification --- two spatial
parameters expressing one predicate, attribute equality in CQL2, no analysis
vocabulary anywhere --- and the probe establishes that no processing endpoint
exists anywhere in ISRO's public stack.

Three things make that a large gap rather than a missing convenience.
\textbf{It is an absent tier}: server-side processing has been an OGC standard
since 2007. \textbf{Peers implement it}: Copernicus publishes 146 processes at a
public endpoint this project enumerates live; Bhoonidhi publishes zero.
\textbf{The cost is measurable}: 208 scenes returned where 16 cover the same
ground --- 92\,\% redundant, 206\,GB ordered where 16\,GB was required --- and
the user cannot discover this, because discovering it takes seven manual steps
including an NP-hard optimisation and a reprojection that is easy to get
silently wrong. So they order everything. Since redundancy rises with archive
depth and this measurement spans three months, 92\,\% is a floor.

The service closing that gap implements OGC API --- Processes Part 1: Core over
the geometry the catalogue already stores: six operations across footprints and
the raster beneath them, synchronous and asynchronous execution, a conformant
job API, and areas in a per-request equal-area projection so that
km\textsuperscript{2} means km\textsuperscript{2}. All 135 compliance tests
pass, and the suite verifies the mathematics rather than only the protocol
shape. Three real defects were found during development, two of them by those
invariant tests.

The sharpest result is the smallest. Thirty scenes match a district-sized area
of interest. \textbf{Two cover it.} Finding that out takes this service nine
milliseconds, and takes Bhoonidhi's users an afternoon they do not spend --- so
instead they download the other twenty-eight.

The gap is one of capability and architecture, not a failing of the data or the
people who publish it. NRSC makes a remarkable volume of Indian Earth
observation genuinely available. The argument is narrow: a catalogue that knows
every footprint's geometry but will not compute with it leaves its hardest work
to every user, one at a time --- and that step, from \emph{which scenes touch my
area} to \emph{which scenes I actually need}, is the one that turns a catalogue
into infrastructure.
